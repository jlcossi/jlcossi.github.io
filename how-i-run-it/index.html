The same method for two products

With many clients, AI made coding faster and delivery didn’t move: the bottleneck moved from writing the work to judging it. I hit the same wall on my own products. So I built the operation around it. Agents do the volume. I own the few decisions that carry risk, and I turn them into rules the system enforces.

It isn’t just my experience. In GitLab’s June 2026 AI Accountability Report, 85% of respondents agreed that AI has shifted the bottleneck from writing code to reviewing and validating it.

  1. Spec firstEach product has one source of truth: a spec complete enough that an agent could rebuild the app from it. The spec is the contract.
  2. Agents buildCode, tests, docs, deploy scripts. Here the volume isn’t the point; the outcome numbers further down are.
  3. Risk-tiered reviewThis is where the bottleneck now lives. At my volume I can read every diff. At yours you can’t, and you shouldn’t. The discipline is deciding which tier a change belongs to before it reaches a human.
  4. Merge is releaseA merge to main deploys in about 30 seconds, self-hosted. The blast radius is small, so the verification happens in the live product.
  5. MeasureEach product has its own track record. Every figure on this page reconciles with it.

The tiers, as applied to this pipeline. The principle: the risk of a change decides how much human attention it gets.

    Reversible, internal

    Reversible, public

Where the judgment sits once they’re live

The same logic runs past the build. I don’t read every Spreadwire verdict at 3am, and I don’t write each Racegaps debrief. I decide the rules that let output through: what counts as a significant move, how long to wait before believing it, which sources may be cited, which facts may come from the model and which may not. The system applies those rules every time, and the track record shows whether they hold.

The cost base stays deliberately small: one server, SQLite, free-tier models with a paid fallback. The running cost per product stays modest, by design.

Worked example 1

Spreadwire

Judgment in a continuous flow

Spreadwire watches prediction-market odds on two elections around the clock: the US 2028 race and the French 2027 presidential. When a price moves sharply, it publishes a sourced verdict within about two minutes: real move, overreaction, or noise. The flow never stops, so the quality gate is the product.

What the AI does

  • Gathers the context around every confirmed move: news headlines from the last hours, how the other candidates moved, spikes in public attention, the week’s price history
  • Judges whether the move is a real reaction to news, an overreaction, or noise, and writes the verdict citing its sources
  • Works at 3am and on Sundays, for about $0.0015 a verdict

What I decide

  • What counts as a sharp move: a multiple of each market’s normal swing, not a fixed number
  • The 90-second re-check: a move that reverts is never published
  • Which sources a verdict may cite, and the three verdicts it may give
  • No usable evidence, no explanation: the move goes out without text rather than with invented text

In your operation

Replace market moves with incoming tickets or order exceptions. Most of the 1,210 alerts didn’t deserve any work: 715 had faded 90 seconds later. Answering each one straight away would have meant 715 explanations to write, then take back. In a service center, that’s the bot that answers every ticket instantly and gets some wrong, and the customer calls back. You pay for the same request twice.

The quality doesn’t come from checking the output afterwards. It comes from three rules set upfront: confirm a signal before working on it, judge it against normal variation rather than a fixed number, and when the evidence isn’t there, say so instead of filling the gap. Result: 190 verdicts, none corrected after publication.

If your question is where your AI is working on noise, and where its mistakes come back to you as new demand, that’s the conversation. Everyone sells automation. Almost nobody shows their exception rate.

Worked example 2

Racegaps

Delivery against a fixed deadline

Racegaps is an F1 second screen: live timing for every car, a strategy engine (undercut windows, tyre wear, where a car rejoins after a stop), the title math, and a written debrief that has to land minutes after the flag. The session starts whether I’m ready or not.

What the AI does

  • Watches twenty cars’ strategies in parallel and explains them in plain language as they unfold
  • Writes the live read during the session and the debrief at the flag

What I decide

  • Facts come only from the timing data. The model writes the prose, never the numbers
  • What the strategy engine is allowed to claim
  • How it’s tuned: by replaying recorded races through the detectors, so every change can be tested against real history

In your engineering team

A race weekend is a release date that never moves. Nobody reviews the debrief before it goes out, so the checking has to happen earlier: in the rules about what the model may say, and in replaying every change against recorded races before the weekend. When something broke, I didn’t just fix the bug. The case went into the replay set, so it can’t come back.

Most teams using AI do the opposite. Code arrives faster, then piles up in review at the end, right before the deadline. If your question is why deadlines keep slipping after AI made the coding faster, that’s the conversation.

What carries over to your teams

Two products with opposite stress profiles, run by the same method, make a pattern rather than an anecdote. Not all of it transfers, and knowing which part does is the work.

None of these practices is new; the best tech teams already use them. What most organizations lack is the system to make them stick: who sets the tiers, who owns the rules, which numbers prove it works.

The principles carry over

  • Name the constraint. After AI, it’s judgment, not typing.
  • The spec as contract. One source of truth that agents and people read the same way.
  • Write judgment down. Turn it into explicit rules with an owner.
  • Tier the review by risk. Decide the tier before a change reaches a human, not after it breaks. Anything irreversible gets an explicit yes.
  • Measure what a skeptic can check. Cost per outcome, not handle time.
  • Keep the cost of running it small. An operation that’s cheap to run never needs permission to continue.

The settings don’t

  • Merge is release. Right for a small self-hosted product, wrong for a bank’s core system.
  • One approver. Your gates need the right people, not one person.
  • My tier boundaries. What counts as T3 in a bank isn’t what counts as T3 on a news site.
  • Thresholds and timings. They come from your data, in your process.

Both conversations start the same way: a look at your current flow.
